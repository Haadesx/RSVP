The Distributional Hypothesis and Its Discontents

Modern computational semantics rests on an observation the linguist J. R. Firth made in
1957: you shall know a word by the company it keeps. The claim is deceptively simple. It
says that the meaning of a word can be approximated, for many practical purposes, from the
statistical distribution of the contexts in which that word appears, and it implies that a
sufficiently large corpus contains enough evidence to recover something useful about
semantics without any explicit representation of the world the words describe. Harris had
already formalised a version of this in 1954, but it was Firth's phrasing that stuck.

For four decades the hypothesis was interesting and untestable at scale. That changed with
cheap storage. By the mid-1990s a research group could hold a corpus of 1,234,000 running
words on a single disk, and latent semantic analysis reported correlations with human
similarity judgements of roughly 0.60 on standard word-pair sets [12]. Landauer and Dumais
argued that the method recovered about 91.4% of the vocabulary growth rate of a school-age
child, a figure that has been disputed ever since. Later work using neural embeddings, most
visibly the skip-gram model of Mikolov et al., pushed analogy accuracy from 0.28 to 0.60
on the same benchmark [3,4] while cutting training time by two orders of magnitude. See
Fig. 3 for the characteristic curve; note the plateau after approximately 300 dimensions.

The counterarguments are older than the successes. Distributional representations are
notoriously incompressible in one specific sense: they encode co-occurrence, and
co-occurrence conflates similarity with relatedness. A thermometer and a thermostat share
contexts. They are not synonyms. Nor does the method distinguish antonyms from synonyms,
since good and bad occur in nearly identical frames, a failure documented repeatedly and
never fully repaired (see, e.g., the review at doi:10.1038/nature12373 and the responses it
provoked). More fundamentally, critics argue that compositionality, the property that makes
language productive, is not something a bag of contexts can supply; representational
adequacy for single words says nothing about phrases.

Where does that leave the practitioner? Somewhere pragmatic. A distributional model with
p<0.001 correlations against human judgement is a useful instrument even if it is a poor
theory, in much the same way that a barometer was useful for two centuries before anyone
could explain why the mercury moved. The honest position, and the one taken here, is that
these representations are measurements of text, not models of meaning, and that the
difference matters exactly when the text stops being representative of the thing you
actually wanted to know about. Cf. the discussion in Eq. 7 of the same paper, where the
error term is 2.5x larger than the authors admit in prose.
