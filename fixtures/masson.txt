Why RSVP Loses to Skimming

In 1983 Michael Masson ran five experiments at the University of Victoria comparing rapid serial visual presentation against ordinary skimming. The materials were 40 passages averaging 133 words, drawn from unrelated Reader's Digest stories. Skimming meant the whole passage on screen for a fixed time, matched exactly to the duration the RSVP condition would take. The comparison was therefore about how the time was spent, not how much of it there was.

Skimming won. On recall questions at 375 and 500 wpm the advantage was 10 percentage points and 8 percentage points respectively, F(1,46) = 8.10. On a one-sentence summary scored against unlimited-time controls, skimming won again, F(1,46) = 9.80, and the number of idea units produced was equal at 1.75 versus 1.73, so the difference was accuracy rather than verbosity.

The third experiment is the one that matters most for a reading tool. Participants were shown the question before and after the passage, and their only task was to find the answer. Skimming beat RSVP with F(1,42) = 19.61, and the interaction with rate approached significance at p < .06, suggesting that the skimming advantage increased as presentation got faster. Going faster made RSVP worse, not better.

There is a further result that does not depend on skimming as a baseline at all. Comparing the same 500 wpm RSVP condition across two experiments, participants who knew the question in advance scored .655 while those recalling from memory scored .421, F(1,42) = 25.12. That is a long-term-memory encoding deficit measured against RSVP itself.

Masson was careful about his own method. He warns twice that skimming is a flawed baseline, because it is difficult to know exactly how skimmers distribute their reading time, and because variations in skimming strategy can influence in an arbitrary fashion which questions show deficits.

The constructive finding is buried in his discussion. He notes that earlier studies which included inter-sentence pauses failed to find any advantage for skimming over RSVP. His own fifth experiment confirms the mechanism from the other direction: adding 500 ms pauses between sentences improved RSVP against itself, F(1,30) = 5.42, lifting general comprehension from .508 to .606. A 1,000 ms pause was no better than 500 ms.

Read plainly, the literature says paused RSVP may reach parity with ordinary skimming, and unpaused RSVP does not. That is a build instruction rather than a marketing claim, and it is why the boundary pause is the highest-leverage feature in this reader.
