# Reading Speed Mechanisms: Is Subvocalization the Bottleneck, and Can We Claim "Read Faster"?

Scope: the **speed mechanism** and the **skimming claim**. Prior findings — RSVP costs verbatim comprehension while preserving gist, costs appear above ~300 delivered wpm, the ORP/pivot heuristic is null — are treated as settled and not re-litigated.

---

## 1. Bottom line

1. **The subvocalization premise is false as stated.** Articulatory suppression is the decisive test, and it damages comprehension while leaving reading *speed* completely unchanged: "None of the manipulations influenced the speed with which the subjects performed the reading task" — [Baddeley, Eldridge & Lewis 1981, QJEP 33A:439–454](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748108400802) (abstract retrieved verbatim via [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1080/14640748108400802); publisher full text paywalled).
2. **Articulation speed does not predict silent reading speed.** ρ = −.03 (corrected −.05, n.s.) between silent reading rate and maximum articulation speed, N=94 Dutch-speaking adults 18–60 tested over Zoom, versus ρ = .28 for reading *aloud* — [Vantieghem & Brysbaert 2023, Psychologica Belgica 63(1)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10360968/).
3. **The real ceiling is amodal language throughput.** Silent reading (269 wpm) equals the fastest listening rate that still supports comprehension (270 wpm), with a steep decline at 315 wpm, same participants, matched texts — [Kuperman et al. 2021, JEP:HPP 47(8)](https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000932). Corroborated by a **serial** lexical-access bottleneck: "there is a bottleneck, such that only one word can subsequently reach higher-level language and decision areas" — [White et al. 2019, PNAS 116(20)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6525533/).
4. **RSVP does not remove inner speech, and nobody has measured it at app-typical rates.** Word-at-a-time presentation still drives voice-selective cortex — [Perrone-Bertolotti et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6621680/) — and the only design crossing RSVP with articulatory suppression concludes RSVP *permits* phonological recoding, blocking only cumulative rehearsal — [AuBuchon et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7810201/).
5. **RSVP loses to ordinary skimming at matched total time** — on recall, on summary accuracy, and worst on the "find the answer you're looking for" task, where skimming beat RSVP with F(1,42)=19.61 and the advantage *grew* with rate — [Masson 1983, Memory & Cognition 11(3):262–274](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03196973.pdf). **This pass read the full primary PDF; §5 now reports Masson's own numbers, not a review's summary.**
6. **RSVP also removes re-reading, and that alone costs comprehension.** A trailing-mask experiment built explicitly to test speed-reading apps: "The reader could read each word only once; regressions … were functionally eliminated. Crucially, the inability to regress affected comprehension negatively" — [Schotter, Tran & Rayner 2014, Psychological Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614531148). This mechanism was absent from all four prior research passes.
7. **Speed-reading training buys speed by spending comprehension**, and most of the gain is reproducible by merely making people aware of their pace: app +22.8%, metacognitive-only control +15.8%, untrained +0.7%, comprehension flat in all three, N=30 German undergraduates — [Klimovich et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9817.12417).
8. **One population where RSVP measurably *helps*: adults with ADHD** — but it is a health-adjacent claim on one unreplicated study, and §7 downgrades it accordingly. [Moussaoui et al. 2025, JINS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41277242/), N=38 per group.
9. **The FTC has litigated this exact claim class.** The 1998 order against "Howard Berg's Mega Reading" found no basis for claiming a program "could teach anyone, including adults; children; and disabled individuals, to significantly increase their reading speed while substantially comprehending and retaining the material" — [FTC, 13 Jan 1998](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/1998/01/informercial-marketers-settle-ftc-charges). The [2016 Lumosity order](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program) requires "competent and reliable scientific evidence before making future claims about any benefits for real-world performance, age-related decline, or other health conditions" — and its complaint names **ADHD** explicitly among those conditions.
10. **The consensus prescription is boring**: "practice reading and … become a more skilled language user" — [Rayner et al. 2016, PSPI 17(1)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26769745/). The same abstract concedes the one thing that *does* work for going faster, and it is not RSVP: "If a thorough understanding of the text is not the reader's goal, then speed reading or **skimming** the text will allow the reader to get through it faster with moderate comprehension."

## 2. Is subvocalization the bottleneck?

**The claim** is that the voice in your head reads at mouth speed, so silencing it lets you read at eye speed. Two things get conflated: (a) peripheral articulatory activity measurable by speech-muscle EMG, and (b) phonological recoding — the abstract sound code. [Besner 1987](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748708401799) draws the line: a phonological code "can be derived from printed English and used for lexical access without interference from suppression," while "operations performed upon a phonological code — e.g. post-assembly phonemic segmentation and deletion, maintenance in working memory — are disrupted by suppression." [Leinenger 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4211933/) adds that pre-lexical phonology activates 30–60 ms after word onset with "no strategic control." You cannot switch off the code that identifies words.

**It is load-bearing, not a habit.** Meta-analysis of phonological co-activation in written-word recognition: corrected d = 0.49 across k = 178 effects — [Cui, Damian & Qu 2025, Psychon Bull Rev](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-025-02718-1) (four meta-analyses; orthographic effects on spoken-word recognition k=93, d=0.61). Readers cannot turn it off even when it hurts them: they falsely accept a homophone of a category member ("meet" for *food*) ~19% of the time vs 3% for an orthographic control ([Rayner et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100615623267) — SECONDHAND; PSPI full text unobtainable, see §9).

**Articulatory suppression — the decisive result.** [Baddeley, Eldridge & Lewis 1981](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748108400802), three experiments: suppression "markedly impaired" detection of anomalous words or word-order errors in prose; a concurrent tapping task did *not* (so it isn't generic dual-task load); unattended irrelevant speech "had no effect on performance." Speed never moved. Baddeley's own conclusion is that subvocalisation "allows the creation of a supplementary articulatory code … particularly suitable for monitoring order information" — i.e. it is a comprehension *asset*, not a tax. **N per experiment is not stated in the retrievable abstract — UNVERIFIED.** [Daneman & Newson 1992, Reading and Writing 4:55–77](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01027072) quantify the cost at "an average decrement of 10–12%," speech-specific rather than general resource competition, while flagging "substantial individual differences in the magnitude of the speech interference effect" (**SECONDHAND — abstract not indexed by OpenAlex, Crossref or Europe PMC; quotations carried forward from a prior pass and not re-verified this pass**). [Slowiaczek & Clifton 1980](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(80)90628-3) localise the damage: suppression impairs "tests that required integration of concepts within or across sentences, as contrasted with tests that required only memory of individual word concepts" — exactly the known RSVP profile (**SECONDHAND, same caveat**). And the manipulation is valid: with N=40 adults making speeded acceptability judgements, suppression abolished the phonological foil effect entirely, and "the word length effect … disappeared under suppression, indicating that the suppression manipulation was highly effective" ([Coltheart, Avons & Trollope 1990](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640749008401227) — abstract verified verbatim).

**The "4,000 wpm inner speech" number misreads its own source.** [Korba 1990](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/inner-voice/1990-korba.pdf) had subjects solve word-completion puzzles and then *retrospectively expand* their compressed inner speech into full sentences. Primary PDF read this pass: the 21 fastest subjects (55% of the group, so N≈38 total) averaged **3.90 elliptical words** and **43.04 extended words** on Trial 1 in a mean 0.62 s window. Dividing gives 6.3 elliptical words/s (**378 wpm — the rate actually experienced**) and 69.4 extended words/s (**4,164 wpm — a reconstruction, not a rate anyone read at**). Korba's own EMG failed to correlate with either count on any of four trials (best r = .294, p = .07; the other seven coefficients ranged −.191 to .129). Measured on full sentences, inner speech is only "slightly, but significantly, faster than the rate of speech aloud" — [Netsell, Kleinsasser & Daniel 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27562695/), N=20 (3M/17F), and the authors themselves warn "Physical measures of speaking rate are needed to validate self-timed measures."

### Conflicts
[Abramson & Goldinger 1997](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03205520) found a ~30 ms lexical-decision cost for phonetically long vs short items at equal orthographic length, larger in slower readers, concluding the code is "inner speech rather than abstract phonological codes." [Vantieghem & Brysbaert 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10360968/) found ρ = −.03 with articulation speed and conclude "our findings are in line with Besner's conclusion that articulation is not involved in silent reading." **For the rate question I trust Brysbaert** — same 94 people, both rates measured directly, 26 years newer. These reconcile: the code can carry phonetic detail without the articulators gating throughput. Separately, [Nalborczyk et al. 2020, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0233282) (N=25 French-speaking female psychology undergraduates, preregistered) could not classify inner-speech content from surface facial EMG at all — 47.2% accuracy, 95% CI [.426, .520], **chance 50%**, versus 84.7% for overt speech — supporting the abstract account and implying surface EMG cannot settle the RSVP question either.

The last two rows matter most. [Brysbaert 2019, 190 studies / 18,573 participants](https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/xynwg) (preprint PDF read this pass) puts adult English silent reading at 238 wpm non-fiction (SD 51.2), 260 wpm fiction, and oral reading at 183 wpm (77 studies, 5,965 participants): "Speech remains understandable to healthy, young participants when compressed to 260 wpm. Furthermore, participant groups unable to attain such a reading rate (children, old adults, second language speakers) also seem unable to process spoken language presented at 260 wpm." Readers already outrun the mouth by ~30% untrained; in syllables per minute the gap nearly vanishes — 422 spm reading vs 400 spm *maximum* articulation ([Vantieghem & Brysbaert 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10360968/)).

**RSVP removes exactly one term that helps — saccades, worth ~250 wpm in a pure word-report task with no comprehension test — while removing one that hurts (regressions) and degrading two more (chunk integration, memory span).**

### Conflicts
[Primativo et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4835101/) conclude "data indicate a speed limit of 300 wpm, which corresponds to the time needed for eye movement execution, i.e., the most time consuming mechanism"; [Rayner et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26769745/) conclude the opposite for comprehension. Both can be true — saccades bind in decoding, memory and integration bind for continuous text read for meaning — but note Primativo's own words, "Text comprehension was not evaluated since it goes beyond the scope of the present work," and that their Exp 6 halves the ceiling with stream length while eye movements are held constant. The same caveat sinks the headline "862 wpm" of [Legge et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2729064/): an exposure time yielding 80% of words *read aloud correctly* on 11-word sentences, no comprehension test (**SECONDHAND — carried from a prior pass, not re-verified this pass**). The number tracks the task, not the reader: 12 normally sighted adults (11 analysed) scored ~158, ~832 or ~1,260 wpm depending only on whether they read aloud (MNREAD), judged true/false, or did lexical decision ([Arango et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7438847/), verified this pass).

## 4. Does RSVP reduce subvocalization?

**Nobody has measured it.** Europe PMC full-text search crossing "rapid serial visual presentation" with "articulatory suppression" returns exactly one paper, and it is a digit serial-recall study. No EMG- or neuroimaging-during-RSVP-*reading* study exists above ~86 wpm. This is a documented gap.

What exists points the other way:

- [Perrone-Bertolotti et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6621680/), N=4 intracranial epilepsy patients (3F, mean age 28.75): words presented 100 ms every 700 ms (**≈86 wpm**) drove 50–150 Hz high-frequency activity in temporal voice-selective areas 400–800 ms after the visual response. **Caveat the doc previously omitted: the response was "strongly modulated by attention" and stronger for attended than ignored words, so the authors read it as top-down, not automatic.** N=4 patients is not a population estimate.
- [Rayner et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100615623267), citing Petrick (1981): "Even people reading sentences via RSVP at 720 wpm appear to generate sound-based representations of the words." (**SECONDHAND — PSPI full text unobtainable; quotation carried forward.**)
- [AuBuchon et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7810201/) is the only design manipulating both. RSVP of eight digits at 250 ms/item was chosen deliberately to "allow participants time to transform each item into either an articulatory and/or phonological assembly while limiting their ability to complete any serial sub-vocal recitations before the presentation of the next item" (quote verified verbatim in the PMC full text). N=80 in the RSVP group, N=46 in the silent-concurrent-articulation group, 126 analysed. The phonological signature (irrelevant-speech effect) was enormous under RSVP (BF > 1×10⁹) and abolished only by real articulatory suppression (BF = 0.32 favouring the null). **Caveat: digit serial recall, not prose.**

**The arithmetic problem:** if the inner-voice response peaks 400–800 ms after word onset, then above ~150 wpm the voice for word *n* overlaps words *n+1* to *n+4*. Whether it truncates, persists, or is replaced is untested — UNVERIFIED, claim neither direction. Surface EMG cannot answer it ([Nalborczyk 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0233282)); it needs intracranial or MEG/EEG auditory-cortex measures during connected-text RSVP. The one study sometimes offered as evidence that training reduces subvocalization measured resting-state connectivity in **9 adolescent female volunteers** and reported no reading-speed or comprehension outcome in its abstract ([Ferguson, Nielsen & Anderson 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/13803395.2014.908825) — **SECONDHAND, abstract only**). It cannot support a product claim.

## 5. RSVP vs skimming — verified against the primary source

**Status: PRIMARY.** The full [Masson 1983 PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03196973.pdf) was downloaded and read this pass. Every F-statistic and table value below is from the paper, not from a review. Values marked "figure read" are estimated from plotted figures and are the only approximate numbers here.

Materials, all five experiments: **40 passages, mean length 133 words (range 101–151), from unrelated *Reader's Digest* stories**, two questions each (one gist, one detail), undergraduates at the University of Victoria, Apple II + video monitor. Skimming = the whole passage on screen for a fixed time matched to the RSVP rate.

Table I general questions, skimming .77/.68 (375) and .67/.58 (500) vs RSVP .69/.56 and .57/.52 → **+10 pp at 375, +8 pp at 500**. Experiment 3 is the product's exact use case and the biggest loss. Masson's reading: conceptual operations "may be distributed over later fixations and even postponed until a syntactic boundary is reached." A metronome cannot honour that; the reader's own irregular pacing can.

**Masson's own caveat, which a critic will find and you should state first.** He says it twice: "this fact … suggests an important limitation of the use of skimming as a baseline for comparison against RSVP performance … it is difficult to know exactly how skimmers distribute their reading time," and later, "This outcome points up a general disadvantage of using skimming as a baseline against which RSVP performance is to be compared. Variations in skimming strategies or ability can influence in an arbitrary fashion the types of questions that will exhibit deficits." The cross-experiment .655 vs .421 comparison (row 4 above) is the one result that does not depend on the skimming baseline at all, and it still indicts RSVP.

**The most product-relevant sentence in the whole paper is about pauses.** Masson notes that earlier studies "that included intersentence pauses … failed to find any advantage for skimming over RSVP" (Chen 1982; Juola et al. 1982; Potter et al. 1980). Read plainly: **paused RSVP may reach parity with skimming; unpaused RSVP does not.** That is a build instruction, not a marketing claim.

### Conflicts
Two studies found no RSVP disadvantage; Masson explains both, verbatim. Potter, Kroll & Harris (1980) used "specially constructed passages that depended on readers' making use of a critical topic word embedded in each passage … only subjects in the RSVP condition were guaranteed exposure to the word." Juola, Ward & McNamara (1982) "typically involved 200- or 300-msec presentations of small groups of words at a fixed location on a screen. This presentation method is virtually a simulation of skimming." **But both of those studies also used inter-sentence pauses, which Masson himself flags as a likely cause of the null** — so the honest reading is that the Potter/Juola nulls are partly a materials artefact and partly a real pause effect. I side with Masson on the unpaused comparison: ordinary prose, exact duration matching, a no-read control fixing the guessing floor, four comparisons across three measures — while noting his own baseline caveat above.

**Skimming itself is weaker than folklore — which does not rescue RSVP.** [Duggan & Payne 2009, JEP:Applied 15(3)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016995), 3 experiments on expository texts with time for half the document: skimming "improved memory for important ideas from a text but did not improve memory of less important details or of inferences," and Exp 2 found "no advantage of skimming over reading the first or second half of every paragraph." The Exp 1 advantage "was dependent on the linkages between pages and, thus, the ease with which participants could navigate through the text" — document structure, which RSVP destroys. Skimming also leans on prior knowledge: [O'Reilly et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797619862276), N=3,534 high-school students on an ecology comprehension test, found a knowledge threshold below which comprehension barely responds to knowledge (β = 0.18) and above which it tracks it strongly (β = 0.81).

**Modern comparisons agree, with one nuance that cuts both ways.** In simulated and actual central vision loss, RSVP produced *far* more reading errors than any other format: **49.31% (SE 8.02) vs 1.12–1.51% for scrolling and static text** in Study 1 (N=15 students, simulated CVL), and **11.39% (SE 2.76) vs 1.35–2.82%** in Study 2 (N=37 with macular degeneration, mean age 77.5) — a 33–44× and 4–8× error multiplier respectively. RSVP was also not the fastest format: 146 wpm vs 163 wpm for multiple-line static in Study 1, and 72 wpm vs 102–105 wpm for static formats in Study 2 ([Akthar et al. 2021, J Vision 21(12):5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8590178/)). *(A prior pass said "~25× higher error rate"; the primary numbers above supersede it. Note also that this is a low-vision population, not the product's.)* [Acklin & Papesh 2017](https://doi.org/10.5406/amerjpsyc.130.2.0183) (abstract verified this pass; full text not retrieved) crossed text difficulty (6th vs 12th grade) with static / 700 wpm / 1,000 wpm: "comprehension was most affected by reading speed: Static text was associated with superior performance, relative to either RSVP reading condition. Furthermore, **slower RSVP speeds yielded better verbatim comprehension, and faster speeds benefited inferential comprehension**" — the one result in the corpus where a faster RSVP rate helps anything, and it helps inference at the expense of detail. Over a 20-minute self-paced session with a real book chapter (Orwell), RSVP users converged to ~200 wpm — no faster than paper — and comprehended worse; "Similar results were published by Ricciardi and Di Nocera (2017)" ([Benedetto et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.12.043), quoted via [Brysbaert 2019](https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/xynwg) — **SECONDHAND**).

Klimovich's eye-tracking adds the mechanism: the speed gain came from "fewer and shorter fixations in measures reflecting **late but not early lexical processing**," and the authors attribute it to "an increase in their awareness of their own reading process" rather than any change in basic reading behaviour.

Where training has been measured against comprehension the trade is explicit — but report both halves. [Carver 1985](https://doi.org/10.2307/747851), N=16 across four superior-reader groups: all groups read easy material at 300–600 wpm when ≥75% comprehension was required; the speed-reading group **did** write the best 100-word summary of a 6,000-word book at the 1,500 wpm condition, but "did not write the best summaries at any of the other rates … and they were the worst at recalling the book's important details at all four rates," concluding speed readers "typically choose to skim at rates higher than 1,000 words per minute and accept the lowered comprehension that accompanies skimming." Two elite readers reading a college text at 15,000–30,000 wpm scored +8/+18/+10 and +4/+16/+10 on a 20-item four-alternative exam scored −80 to +80 where a blank paper scores ~0 and **+14 or less is a failing grade** — "both speed-readers would have failed the college exam. In fact, there is little reason to believe that either speed-reader would ever pass the exam, since neither reader exhibited improvement with successive readings" ([Homa 1983](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758%2FBF03329973.pdf), primary PDF read this pass). The best-powered study of trained speed readers — lab-verified ~10.5× speed (11,280 vs 1,070 characters/min) — found **no** comprehension advantage on unfamiliar Japanese expository essays: 64.4% vs 61.4% n.s., **3-alternative MC, chance 33.3%**, N=19 vs 19 ([Sun et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11365886/)).

**On RSVP practice specifically: no published multi-session study exists in normal adults with a control group and comprehension outcomes.** The nearest is [Chung 2011, IOVS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3053100/) — six weekly sessions, **6** observers (mean age 73.8; 4 AMD, 2 Stargardt), +53% RSVP speed (range 34–70%), but critical print size and acuity unchanged and the author conceding "it is possible that improvements in RSVP reading speed may not generalize to page-reading." The Dingler et al. 2015 paper often cited for practice effects is about **kinetic text guiding eye movements**, not RSVP, and its abstract is publisher-elided ([Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/2735711.2735796)) — UNVERIFIED as support for RSVP practice.

## 7. Defensible marketing claims

**Be straight: "read faster because we remove subvocalization" is not supportable and should be abandoned.** False on mechanism (§2), false on RSVP's effect on inner speech (§4), and the specific claim class the FTC prosecuted in [Howard Berg's "Mega Reading"](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/1998/01/informercial-marketers-settle-ftc-charges), where the Commission found Berg "had no basis for claiming that his program could teach anyone, including adults; children; and disabled individuals, to significantly increase their reading speed while substantially comprehending and retaining the material, and the claim is false." The plain "read faster with the same understanding" claim is also unsupportable — the evidence is uniformly a speed/comprehension trade.

Every claim below was rewritten under one test: *could a critic holding this dossier call it misleading?* Three of the four survived only after being weakened.

**Claim A — the ADHD claim. Real, but downgraded from "marketable" to "citable."**

> Safe form: *"There is early published evidence that adults with ADHD comprehend one-word-at-a-time presentation better than normal text. One study, 2025, not yet replicated — [link]. We are not claiming this is a treatment, and it is not a claim about speed."*

> **Do not ship:** "works better if you have ADHD," "designed for ADHD readers," or anything implying improvement in study, work or academic outcomes.

Source: [Moussaoui, Siddiqi, Cheung & Niemeier 2025, JINS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41277242/), N=38 ADHD and N=38 neurotypical undergraduates; three conditions (FULL text, PACED one-word-at-a-time in layout, RSVP centre-screen); **~250 ms/word ≈ 240 wpm**; short paragraphs, one **four-alternative multiple-choice** question each, **chance 25%**, two minutes to answer. Verified from the publisher page: "ADHD participants improved by 6.96% whereas controls got worse by 5.82% thus amounting to a relative benefit of 12.78%," matching the abstract's "by almost 13% relative to neurotypical controls, who showed comprehension difficulties using the RSVP mode."

Why it had to be weakened — four things a critic would say, all of which are true:
1. **It is a health claim about a clinical population.** The [2016 Lumosity order](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program) requires "competent and reliable scientific evidence before making future claims about any benefits for real-world performance, age-related decline, or other health conditions," and the complaint lists **ADHD by name** among the conditions Lumosity was charged over. One online-ahead-of-print study is not that standard.
2. **Berg's order specifically covered "disabled individuals."** Marketing a reading-speed product on a disability benefit is the precise fact pattern of the 1998 case.
3. **It is not a speed claim.** The benefit was measured at ~240 wpm — *below* the 250–300 wpm the app would default to, and far below anything advertised as speed reading.
4. **The per-condition percentages are not verifiable.** A prior pass quoted "ADHD ≈68% vs ≈62%; controls ≈59% vs ≈64%"; those numbers **could not be recovered from Table 1** this pass and are now withdrawn as UNVERIFIED. Only 6.96 / 5.82 / 12.78 are sourced, and the paper does not disambiguate whether these are percentage points of accuracy or relative percentages.

**Claim B — the pacing claim. Survives, with the numbers reattributed.**

> "A pacer, not a superpower. It stops you dawdling and re-reading. In the only controlled trial of a commercial speed-reading app, the app group gained about 23% — and a group taught only to notice their own reading pace gained about 16%."

Backed by [Klimovich et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9817.12417): app 193→237 wpm, metacognitive-only 194→225, control 194→195, comprehension unchanged in all three. Caveats that must not be dropped from the copy: **N=30 total, 10 per cell**, German-speaking undergraduates, comprehension near ceiling, **the trained app is not identified in the paper and may not have been RSVP-based**, and the group means were computed from posted OSF data, not read out of the article. Do not present the 23% as *our* app's number — it is someone else's app, and the honest point of the claim is that most of it is free.

**Claim C — the bounded-rate claim. Weakened; "no measurable comprehension cost" is removed.**

> "Set the pace up to 300 words per minute — above the 238 wpm an average adult reads non-fiction. In the one study that tested it, RSVP at 250, 300 and 350 wpm was statistically indistinguishable from ordinary reading; above that it was not."

Baseline: [Brysbaert 2019](https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/xynwg) (238 wpm non-fiction, 190 studies, 18,573 participants). Ceiling: **Di Nocera, Ricciardi & Juola 2018** — note the author order, which a prior pass had reversed — "Rapid serial visual presentation: degradation of **inferential** reading comprehension as a function of speed," [DOI 10.1504/IJHFE.2018.096118](https://doi.org/10.1504/IJHFE.2018.096118), abstract retrieved verbatim from the [publisher's article-info page](https://www.inderscience.com/info/inarticle.php?artid=96118): "We tested the method using 209 participants divided into six groups. The groups included traditional reading, and RSVP reading at rates of 250, 300, 350, 400, and 450 wpm. No significant differences were found in comprehension for normal reading and RSVP reading at rates of 250, 300 and 350 wpm. However, higher rates produced significantly lower comprehension scores."

Why "no measurable comprehension cost" was cut: it is a **null result** presented as evidence of safety, from ~35 participants per cell in a single between-subjects study whose comprehension instrument (item count, literal/inferential split, **chance level**) is UNVERIFIED — the full text is behind a 403. The title itself says the degradation is *inferential*, implying the paper distinguishes literal from inferential and the abstract does not report which. And it conflicts with [Acklin & Papesh 2017](https://doi.org/10.5406/amerjpsyc.130.2.0183), where static text beat RSVP outright. Advertise 300, phrase it as "one study found no difference," never as "no cost."

**Claim D — the exposure claim. Keep only as mechanism, never as benefit.**

> Safe form: *"Every word passes in front of you — you can't skip a line by accident."*

> **Do not ship:** any version that implies this produces better understanding.

The mechanism is real and is Masson's own explanation of the Potter et al. (1980) null: "only subjects in the RSVP condition were guaranteed exposure to the word." But guaranteed exposure is not comprehension, and Masson's own experiments are the proof — RSVP guarantees exposure and *still* loses on every measure ([Masson 1983](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03196973.pdf)). Worse, exposure is not even guaranteed *perception*: RSVP produced 49.31% reading errors under simulated central vision loss versus 1.12–1.51% for every other format ([Akthar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8590178/)).

**Claim E — the small-screen claim. New, and the cleanest one available.**

> "Read a full article on a watch face or a phone widget. One word at a time needs almost no screen."

This is the framing the RSVP literature itself uses. Di Nocera, Ricciardi & Juola open with it: "There is increasing interest in the readability of text presented on small digital screens … Comparative studies have indicated that RSVP is perhaps the best method of presenting text in a limited space" ([abstract](https://www.inderscience.com/info/inarticle.php?artid=96118)). It is a **space** claim, not a speed claim, and no result in this dossier contradicts it — which is exactly why it is defensible.

## 8. What this means for the product

- **Pause at sentence and clause boundaries by default — this is the single highest-leverage feature in the dossier.** It reliably improves RSVP against itself ([Masson Exp 5, F(1,30)=5.42](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03196973.pdf)); readers impose a ~2 Hz chunk rhythm when given the clock ([Lo et al. 2023, N=181](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10613263/)); and Masson notes that every prior study using inter-sentence pauses "failed to find any advantage for skimming over RSVP." 500 ms was as good as 1,000 ms.
- **Default the slider to ~250–300 wpm, not 500+.** Parity holds to 350 and breaks at 400 in the one study that tested it ([Di Nocera, Ricciardi & Juola 2018](https://doi.org/10.1504/IJHFE.2018.096118)); the ADHD benefit was shown at ~240 wpm ([Moussaoui 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41277242/)); the word-selective cortical response peaks at a 4 Hz base rate ([Marchive et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39785667/)).
- **Make rewind cheap, prominent and one-keystroke.** This is not a nicety; it restores the single function RSVP removes. Blocking re-reading measurably hurts comprehension ([Schotter, Tran & Rayner 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614531148)), 10–15% of normal reading eye movements are regressions ([Sturt & Kwon 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6132172/)), and PSPI puts it plainly: "saccades are used to naturally move to the parts of text in most need of processing and to move back to material that has not been adequately understood. Eliminating them generally impairs the recall accuracy of material presented at faster speeds" ([PSPI summary of Rayner et al. 2016](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/speed_reading.html)).
- **Slow proportionally to sentence length, or chunk explicitly** — rate collapses past memory span, 896 → 524 wpm from 6- to 12-word streams ([Primativo Exp 6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4835101/)).
- **Do not build "skim mode" on RSVP.** Build it on structure: keep the page, extract headings and first sentences, highlight. First-half-of-every-paragraph matches human skimming ([Duggan & Payne 2009 Exp 2](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016995)). AI faceted highlights cut information-seeking time from 117.7 s to 94.3 s — **~20%, F(1,126)=4.17, p<.05** — with accuracy unchanged (0.80 vs 0.76, F(1,126)=0.22, p=.64) and perceived effort unchanged, N=19 ([Scim, IUI '23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.04561)). Two caveats the design must respect: the benefit appeared **only when the answer was inside a highlight** (Task 2: 93.8 s vs 127.3 s, F(1,54)=4.84, p<.05) and vanished when it was not (Task 3, F(1,54)=0.58, n.s.); and the authors note participants were mostly **scanning, not skimming**. *(A prior pass reported "~26% with accuracy unchanged" — that was the Task-2-only figure presented as the overall.)*
- **Ship a cheap comprehension check.** Readers explicitly instructed to hunt for planted contradictions still missed "up to 51% of them," with the proportion of illusions rising from .11 to .63 as texts got longer ([Glenberg, Wilkinson & Epstein 1982](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03202442.pdf); main-experiment N not stated in the retrievable text, subjects run in groups of 6–9). Separately, digital reading loses to paper (g = −0.21, 171,055 participants) and **the gap widens under time pressure** — "the paper-based reading advantage increased in time-constrained reading compared to self-paced reading" ([Delgado et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003)). *(A prior pass cited this as evidence about metacognitive calibration; it is not — it is a paper-vs-screen meta-analysis. Its actual relevance is worse for RSVP: RSVP is the screen-plus-time-pressure cell, the worst one in the analysis.)*
- **Position on space, not speed.** "A whole article in a widget" is defensible (§7 Claim E); "read 2× faster" is not.
- **Generate your own evidence.** In-app A/B logging of rate × comprehension is cheap, is the only route to a substantiated claim under the [Lumosity standard](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program), and fills a gap five research passes have now confirmed is empty.

Claim D was demoted from a benefit to a mechanism statement, because Masson's own data show guaranteed exposure coexisting with worse comprehension, and Akthar shows exposure is not even perception. Claim B survived largely intact but now names the sample as N=30 with 10 per cell and flags that the trained app may not have been RSVP. One claim was **added**: Claim E, the small-screen framing, which is how the RSVP literature itself motivates the technique and which nothing in this dossier contradicts.
